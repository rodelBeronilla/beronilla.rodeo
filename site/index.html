About

Two halves of the same habit.

Whatever it says on the contract, the job is the same: find the distance between what a system is supposed to do and what it actually does, then close it or say plainly that it's open. Today that's compliance and assurance for healthcare technology at TELUS Health. Before that it was telecom at Rogers and electronics manufacturing at Celestica.

I like the part most people find tedious. A control, a backup, a failover — it either works on a bad day or it doesn't, and the only way to know is to go and look at the evidence. Writing that up so the next person can follow the reasoning without sitting through a meeting is most of the work, and doing it honestly is the whole point.

The other half of me builds. There's a server in my house running twenty-six services that I wrote, wired and keep alive, and this page is drawn by it. That's not a hobby I keep separate from the day job. It's a lot easier to ask a team for evidence when you maintain the same kind of system yourself, and a lot harder to accept a vague answer once you've had to give a real one.

Skills

The same five things, whatever the job is called.

  • Telling what something claims from what it does

    Establishing what a system really does, as opposed to what its documentation, its diagram or its owner says it does. In compliance that means testing a control against what the standard requires. The exercise doesn't change when the claim is an SLA, a runbook or a README.

  • Turning a long list into an order of work

    Any honest review finds more than a team can act on at once. The useful output is a sequence: what to fix first, what can wait, and what it actually costs if it waits. Risk modelling against ISO 27002 and NIST 800-53 is the formal version; the judgement is the part that travels.

  • Making complicated things legible

    Evidence organised, owners named, reasoning written down so the next person can follow it alone. I would rather spend an extra hour writing it properly than answer the same question four times, and it is the difference between an audit that is a formality and one that is a fire drill.

  • Building and operating, not only reviewing

    Twenty-six services on a server in my house: Python services I wrote and test, metrics and alerting, encrypted backups, network paths that fail closed. I'm the only person on call for it. Running something end to end changes how you read someone else's architecture, and what you believe when they tell you it's fine.

  • Working where mistakes are expensive

    Healthcare data under PHIPA, PIPEDA and HIPAA; telecom; electronics manufacturing under NIST 800-171. Regulated rooms where "we think it's fine" is not an answer anyone can sign, and where the cost of being wrong lands on someone who never chose the system.

Standards and frameworks I've worked in, if you want the detail:

ISO/IEC 27001:2022The international standard for an information security management system. Certification follows an accredited audit against the Annex A controls the organization declares applicable.
ISO/IEC 27002:2022The companion guidance to 27001: how to implement each control, organized into organizational, people, physical and technological themes.
ISO/IEC 27017Cloud-specific security controls layered on ISO 27002, with responsibilities split between cloud providers and their customers.
SOC 2 Type 2An independent auditor's report on how a service organization's controls operated over a period, against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria.
NIST SP 800-53The US federal catalogue of security and privacy controls, used well beyond government as a baseline to build and assess against.
NIST SP 800-171Requirements for protecting controlled unclassified information in non-federal systems, and the basis of CMMC for defence suppliers.
PHIPAOntario's Personal Health Information Protection Act: how health information custodians may collect, use and disclose personal health information.
PIPEDACanada's federal private-sector privacy law, the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act, built on ten fair information principles.
HIPAAThe US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act; its Privacy and Security Rules set the baseline for protected health information.

How I work

Four rules I actually keep.

  • A green dashboard is not evidence.

    Every service I run can report healthy while the system quietly fails at the job it exists to do. So I go and look at the thing that shows work actually happening — the metric, the record, the response — and I ask other people for the same.

  • "We've always done it this way" is not an argument.

    Neither is "that was already decided." I'll take a good reason over precedent every time, and that includes arguing against decisions I made myself last year. The point is to be right at the end, not consistent along the way.

  • Don't experiment on people who are using it.

    When something is live and someone depends on it, investigation stays read-only until there's a plan and a way back. Curiosity is not a good reason to restart something at dinner time, and "let's see what happens" is not a test.

  • One change, one reason.

    If I find a second problem while fixing the first, it gets written down rather than folded in. Changes that quietly do two things are the ones nobody can review, and the ones nobody can undo when they go wrong at three in the morning.

Built

Things I made and still look after.

  • A server that runs my household, and my friends'

    Dell PowerEdge, Docker Compose, Python, Prometheus, Grafana, restic

    Twenty-six services on a retired enterprise box in my house, shared with eight people: family and friends across several time zones. Python services I wrote and test, network paths that fail closed rather than leak, metrics and alerting over all of it, encrypted backups, and a watchdog living outside Docker so something survives when everything else stops.

    I'm the only person on call. Someone I know is usually watching something on it right now, which turns out to be a stricter test than any staging environment I've been given at work: there is no maintenance window that nobody notices. Next job is moving it off Docker and onto the bare metal, so twenty-eight cores stop pretending to be a laptop.
