Principles

Four, and where each came from.

The data comes before the answer.

I go at a problem like a researcher. I will push an approach further than most people would, and when the evidence finally refuses I stop arguing with it, ask the people around me, and take another route. These four read the same at work and outside it.

  • Say only what is true

    Claim what I did, not what the team did. Where the work was shared, say shared. The bigger claim is easy to make and expensive to defend later.

    A colleague led the NIST remediation at Celestica and I supported it. The Experience page says supported, because that is what happened.

  • Get the question right

    The costly mistakes are the ones where the right thing gets built for the wrong requirement. I ask until there is no ambiguity left, then commit to it properly.

    At Rogers the security requirements were settled in workshops with network, IT and vendor teams before there was a design to defend.

  • Start from the data

    Reaching for a solution early is how you end up defending one. I would rather sit in the evidence until the shape of the problem is obvious, and let that choose the answer.

    Moving a vendor risk dataset off a platform being retired meant discovering its endpoints and reconstructing how its tables related before a single record could move.

  • Take something from everything

    No role has been a dead end. The ones that were uncomfortable at the time are the ones I still draw on.

    A part time job taught me to write the procedure down. An internship taught me to learn from whoever is sitting next to me. The CISSP was self study around a full time job.

Projects

Two in use, one still an idea.

  • FantasyScout

    Python, dbt, Databricks, Cloudflare, Astro. Open source, MIT

    A WNBA data platform, built in the open. Ingestors pull from public sources into a lakehouse, dbt models shape it into a star schema down to play by play and shot locations, and a quality gate decides whether a build is fit to publish. The gold layer is exported and served from the edge, with notebooks and a browser SQL console over the same data, and apps on top for people who would rather not write a query.

    Source on GitHub

  • NIST OSCAL assessment platform

    Planned. Nothing built yet

    Compliance still travels as documents: a catalogue here, a system security plan there, evidence in a folder, results in a spreadsheet. OSCAL is NIST’s machine readable form for all of it. The idea is to keep catalogues, profiles, plans and results as data, so a control can be traced from the catalogue through its implementation to the evidence behind it, and assessment results can be generated and checked rather than written by hand. I want it because I keep crosswalks between frameworks and gather the same evidence twice, which only stays manual while a control is a paragraph instead of a record.
