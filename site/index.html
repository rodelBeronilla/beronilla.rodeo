Principles

Four, and where each came from.

The data comes before the answer.

I work like a researcher. I will back an approach further than most people would, and when the evidence finally says no I stop defending it, go to the people around me, and take another route. These four hold whether the problem is at work or not.

  • Say only what is true

    Give others their share of the credit and own my part, even when a stronger claim would read better.

    At Celestica the NIST remediation was led by a colleague and supported by me. The Experience page says supported, because that is what happened.

  • Understand it first

    Get the context and the requirements, and keep asking until they are clear. Then commit and follow through.

    At Rogers the security requirements were argued out in workshops with network, IT and vendor teams before there was a design to defend.

  • Start from the data

    Understand the problem through its evidence before reaching for an answer, and hold the approach loosely enough that the evidence can still change it.

    Moving a vendor risk dataset off a dying platform meant finding the endpoints and rebuilding the relationships between its tables before one record could move.

  • Take something from everything

    Every role leaves something worth carrying. The parts that were uncomfortable usually left the most.

    A part time job taught me to write the procedure down, an internship taught me to learn from the people next to me, and the CISSP was self study around a full time job.

Projects

  • FantasyScout

    Python, dbt, Databricks, Cloudflare, Astro. Open source, MIT

    A WNBA data platform, built in the open. Ingestors pull from public sources into a lakehouse, dbt models shape it into a star schema down to play by play and shot locations, and a quality gate decides whether a build is fit to publish. The gold layer is exported and served from the edge, with notebooks and a browser SQL console over the same data, and apps on top for people who would rather not write a query.

    Source on GitHub

  • NIST OSCAL assessment platform

    Planned. Nothing built yet

    Compliance still travels as documents: a catalogue here, a system security plan there, evidence in a folder, results in a spreadsheet. OSCAL is NIST’s machine readable form for all of it. The idea is to keep catalogues, profiles, plans and results as data, so a control can be traced from the catalogue through its implementation to the evidence behind it, and assessment results can be generated and checked rather than written by hand. I want it because I keep crosswalks between frameworks and gather the same evidence twice, which only stays manual while a control is a paragraph instead of a record.
